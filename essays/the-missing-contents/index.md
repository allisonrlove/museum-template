Every object in this collection is incomplete, and the incompleteness is not damage. It is the ordinary condition of surviving. The colour has gone off one, the contents out of another, and the practice that made sense of the third has left no record at all. An essay can say what an object page cannot: that the absence is itself evidence, and of what.

## The substance that evaporated

The [Cosmetic Jar]({{ site.baseurl }}/objects/cosmetic-jar) is a container built to a standard its contents must have justified: worked glass, a gilt mount, a lid made to seal. Whatever it held was worth that expense and has not survived a day of it.

{% include typography/pullquote.html
  text="A trade in perishable goods leaves behind only the packaging. The jar is the receipt."
%}

This is the ordinary asymmetry of material evidence. Ceramics, stone, and glass outlast textiles, pigments, oils, and food by orders of magnitude, so a collection assembled from what survives will always over-represent the durable. Saying so on the page is more honest than letting the silence pass for absence.

## The paint that wore off

The [Head of the Buddha]({{ site.baseurl }}/objects/buddha-head) is catalogued as stucco *with traces of paint*, which is a museum's way of saying it was once a coloured object and is now a grey one. Every photograph of it records a state its makers never saw.

The unfinished reverse says something too: the head was made to be seen from the front, set into a wall or a niche. What is missing here is not only the paint but the building.

## The belief that left no text

The dragons on the [Bowl with Dragons]({{ site.baseurl }}/objects/bowl-with-dragons) have been read as a device against eclipses. That reading rests on iconography and analogy rather than on anyone writing down what they thought the bowl was for. The object is firm evidence; the practice around it is an inference, and an essay is the right place to say which is which.
